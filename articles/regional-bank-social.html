Too much content but nothing to say.

A Fortune 500 finance brand published across four business lines constantly. But its social team had no way to decide what to post month-to-month.

ClientFortune 500 bank, agency side
RoleContent strategist, social strategy
CadenceMonthly content plan
NoteClient and agency unnamed

Social and editorial lived side by side.

While a content-rich site supported four distinct business lines and their audiences, the two spaces lived in silos. The marketing manager wanted that content to work harder for the business.

More owned content than almost anyone in the category.

Podcasts, market commentary, small business guidance, long-form web content, published regularly across four business lines: there was no shortage of meaningful content. But a busy social manager doesn't have time to sift that inventory for what's immediately postable and shouldn't be making strategic messaging decisions on the fly.

But publishing isn't programming.

No mechanism existed to turn the vast content inventory into a sustainable, targeted social cadence.

Most content-heavy organizations have a similar problem.

Social managers are drinking from a firehose. Too often, nobody owns the step between the two: content teams produced assets, social teams filled a calendar, and everything published is promoted for a day and then treated as done. Evergreen content collects dust, and no distribution plan takes into account the realities of the audience needs.

What's in the content inventory?

Repurposing

Repurposing / Atomization Matrix

Maps a single source asset against the formats and channels it can be broken into, so one piece of work becomes a planned set of many rather than an afterthought.

View on Content Strategy Library

The monthly question stopped being "what should we say?" and became "what do we already have?" This matrix is the conversion step that lacked an owner.

People and pillars and podcasts. (Oh my!)

When the message was right but the format wasn't sticky enough, a bit of content transformation bolstered reach and engagement. We clipped audio, captioned it, and packaged as video (via "audiogram" visuals) so social algorithms treat it as video rather than a static post.

The matrix let us know what we could say, not what we should say.

The judgment layer necessitates up to date financial research (rates, small business conditions, regional economies) and identifying which of it mattered to which pillar's audience right now.

What came of it

After taking over this ongoing strategy, I was brought onto their next major campaign rollout to help build its messaging matrix.

What I'd do differently today

Name the layer that was already working.

Programming

Hero-Hub-Help

Three tiers by frequency and purpose: rare tentpoles, a recurring series, always-on answers.

View on Content Strategy Library

The weekly recommendations were already a Hub layer, the recurring series that keeps a feed alive week to week. Across all four business lines the pattern was identical: Hub was running, Hero activated only when something in the market forced it to rather than stemming from a timed plan, and Help, the always-on answers to the questions customers actually arrive with, was thin overall.
