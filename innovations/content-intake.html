Visual Content Intake

A campaign brief lived in one document with 214 fields, ten sections, and every answer typed into the same line as the question it answered. This is what replaced it.

The problem

Great creative work is like a must be aimed at the right problem. Valid ideas originate from creative teams internally but also from their stakeholders and customers, and often a synthesis of both. But requesters don’t often know how to say what they want effectively, which is why creative briefs exist.

The problem within the problem

But project briefs can easily become obstructive rather than enabling. A painful request process can be a barrier to requesting work -- which we do not want in an era of “I’ll just have my favorite AI do this” -- and perhaps worse, a barrier to understanding the request. Asking creatives to sift through a ten page wall of text is an operational absurdity.

In one work scenario I observed, the regular request form was perhaps as bad as it could have been, but it had gotten there understandably. Each development was bolted on without regard to usability or bloat. The end result was user-hostile:

  • A huge editable document in a nonstandard format inside a popular task platform
  • Users needed to remember to duplicate the template and fill it out each time
  • Instead of standard form fields, requesters had to write in the same space as the questions in a different color for visual separation

All resources from whom work was being requested were exposed to the ENTIRE document and had to find the parts that were relevant to their work, and ignore everything else.

The solution that didn’t help

Because we live in the era we live in, the first solution was to simply throw generative AI at the problem. “Copilot, make this a nice, structured project brief.” The result was AI introducing a host of ambiguous wording, extraneous verbiage, and y’know -- em-dashes. Work actually became more difficult. Creative resources were exhausted trying to extract meaningful information.

The specifics were the first thing to go. A key message written as “you cannot automate a judgment nobody has written down” came back as “technology and human judgment work in tandem.” Mid-market controllers closing 200–800 invoices a month in spreadsheets became “finance and accounting professionals across a range of organizational profiles.” Requested sizes turned into a promise that specifications would be confirmed during kickoff.

The requester had also asked, in writing, that nobody use the word “seamless.” The generated brief used it twice.

The actual solution

Requesters needed ease of use. Resources needed clarity in the output.

Treating internal stakeholders as valued customers is an overlooked approach to harmonious work. They deserve a sensible UX. So that’s what I designed: I turned the request form and all its branching if-then dependencies into a clickable UI with graphical UI elements where possible. After a complete survey of all the tactics supported by the team’s expertise, a menu bloomed, allowing stakeholders to rapidly request what they want and fill in the details, while ensuring they provided the required details.

This kept requesters accountable to pass critical info on to the team, and think through marketing support in a structured way.

But waht about the output? In its ideal state, each resource only receives a write-up of what they need, but it includes everything they need. Dependencies and other contextual FYIs are generated in the resulting documentation. It is as graphical as necessary while retaining a history of all dates and specifications.

It cuts out entirely unnecessary fat from a process that already hasa bad diet.

The model

Working. Pick your content types, answer only what they ask, and submit to see what each resource receives. Social, Creative, and Email are pre-selected to show the dependency behaviour.

How I made it

The process itself does not require generative AI, as it’s little more than a structured workflow and a PDF producer. Designing and coding it does, though. I used Claude Design and Code to design and build it respectively.

Implementation is highly variable depending on where clients host it an dwhat platforms they use for internal work. At the time, there was no obvious way to splice it directly inot the task platform for the client. However, the task platform rolled out a solution for exactly this use case 6 months later, strongly validating the project.
